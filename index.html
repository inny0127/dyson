Built from Mercury, worked out from physics

A Dyson Swarm, Made Real

One compute sail, seen from a kilometre away at the Sun's true brightness. It is black because it is a good solar cell.

Most pictures of a Dyson swarm show the Sun wrapped in glittering machinery. We tried to work one out properly: mine Mercury, turn it into solar panels that are also computers, and fly them around the Sun until there is no rock left. The swarm we ended up with is made of nearly black sheets, wraps most of the sky round the Sun, and catches most of its light. Earth keeps its temperature behind a sunshade, and pays for it in sunlight and rain. Every photograph and figure here comes from one physics model, and if you change an assumption the whole page recomputes.

The short version

Words and units used on this page
Share of the Sun's light
The fraction of everything the Sun puts out that the sails catch. The finished swarm catches most of it.
AU
The distance from Earth to the Sun, 150 million km.
W/m² of extra heat
How much more energy Earth absorbs, for every square metre of its surface. Doubling the CO₂ in the air adds about 3.9.
FLOP/s
Calculations per second. One 2025 AI chip does about 10¹⁵; all of them together, about 3×10²².
lux
How brightly the ground is lit. A moonless night is 0.001, a full Moon 0.25, a lit street 10 to 20.
cd/m²
How bright a surface or a patch of sky looks. A dark night sky is 0.0002, the sky at dusk 1 to 100, a white page under office light about 100.
Magnitude
Astronomers' scale for points of light. Lower is brighter, and 5 steps is a factor of 100. The faintest stars you can see are +6, Venus −4.5, the full Moon −12.7, the Sun −26.7.
Kelvin (K)
Temperature counted up from absolute zero. A room is about 295 K, a chip at its limit about 360 K, and 1,650 K is orange-hot.
Ecliptic
The path the Sun and planets follow across the sky.
L1 and L3
L1 is the balance point 1.5 million km from Earth toward the Sun, where a sunshade can hover. L3 is the point on Earth's orbit directly behind the Sun, where sails would mirror light straight back at Earth, so they stay clear of it.
Counter-sun
The point in the sky exactly opposite the Sun. Every sail beyond you in that direction mirrors the Sun back at you at once.
Thermal infrared
The heat glow that thermal cameras see, here the 8–14 µm band. Warmer things are brighter.

A sheet, not a box

Each satellite is a huge, nearly black sheet of solar film, thinner than paper, which is also the computer.

· The first sail

The panel is the computer

The chips are spread through the film, so the film's own surface gets rid of their heat.

· The first sail

Why the chips are not in one box

Gathering the chips into one box would need pumps, pipes and radiators that weigh more than the chips. Spreading them out avoids all of it.

· The heat wall

Where the rock comes from

Mercury has rock to spare. What it runs short of is a way to get rid of the heat of digging it up and throwing it into space.

Chip fabs set the pace, so they are the first thing to leave Mercury. In space a fab needs no building, no clean air and no water. The free vacuum alone removes about 60% of a fab's electricity and a third or more of its equipment. Mercury has almost no hydrogen, so the chemistry has to run dry, on chlorine, oxygen, zinc and magnesium.

· Growing

How fast it can be built, and where it stops

For decades there is nothing to see. Then the swarm grows fast, until Mercury cannot shed heat any faster.

· Earth

Earth keeps its temperature, behind a shade

Every watt the sails catch ends up as heat, and some of it reaches Earth. A sunshade cancels it, at a price paid in sunlight and rain.

After the swarm · Earth by design

A climate chosen region by region?

With this much energy, sunlight on Earth becomes something you can place. Temperature follows it closely; rain does not.

· Inside the swarm

A sky with two windows

From any sail the sky glows evenly in every direction, except where the swarm leaves gaps: one round the line to Earth, one behind the Sun.

The finished swarm is one thin layer at 0.95 AU, about 600,000 km thick, and its sails are packed close: neighbours are about 38 km apart. From any one of them the sky glows evenly in every direction. Away from the Sun you see the sunlit fronts of the sails farther out, about 33 cd/m², as bright as a clear sky in early twilight. Toward the Sun you look past the dark backs of the sails just inside you, across the empty middle of the swarm, to its far side, where every sail faces you: 48 cd/m². The Sun itself, seen through the sails sunward of you, is a third dimmer than it would be.

The only dark sky is in the two gaps. Look toward Earth and the gap round the line from the Sun to Earth is a black window about 23° across, with Earth shining in the middle of it. Beyond the Sun, the gap round L3 is a smaller one.

How this sky was drawn

The sails form one layer at 0.95 AU, about 600,000 km thick, covering 88% of the sky round the Sun outside the two gaps. Each sail faces the Sun, so from any point you see a sail's lit front if it is farther from the Sun than you, and its dark back if it is nearer. Along every line of sight we add up the fronts it passes, dimmed by the backs in the way and by the sails that shade them from the Sun. Lines of sight toward the Sun leave the layer, cross the empty inside, and meet the layer again from the inside, where every sail faces you. Sails within 3,000 km are drawn one by one.

How this was made

The model, the Blender scenes and the research behind them are in the project repository: docs/architecture.md (why sails and pods), docs/future-tech.md (what hardware actually flies), docs/earth.md (what Earth allows), docs/data/ (every study's numbers and sources) and src/model/hybrid.js (the model, with tests). Photographs are path traced in Blender Cycles, and the views from orbit ray-cast by our own renderer, at the true brightness of every light source; thermal images map 8–14 µm band radiance to a fixed false-colour scale.
