From inside the swarm

A sky of sails, and a second sun

The swarm Earth allows is sparse. Neighbouring sails are about 300 km apart, and from any one of them the others are points of light: about two dozen within 1,000 km shine like the brightest stars, and tens of thousands more fill the sky to the limit of the eye. Together they light the whole sky away from the Sun to about 0.8 cd/m², the brightness of the sky in early twilight on Earth.

Every sail faces the Sun and reflects a little of it straight back. So when you look directly away from the Sun, every sail beyond you flashes at once. Their reflections add up to a counter-sun at the antisolar point: brighter than the full Moon, violet from the film's anti-reflection coating, inside a halo 15° across from the curled edges of the film panels. The design spec's own glint numbers produce it; nobody designed it.

The mine and the fabs

Mercury, before and after

Chip fabs set the pace, so they are the first thing to leave Mercury. A vacuum fab in space needs no building, no clean air and no water: free vacuum removes about 60% of a fab's electricity and a third or more of its equipment. Mercury has almost no hydrogen, so the chemistry runs dry, on chlorine, oxygen, zinc and magnesium.

How this was made

The model, the Blender scenes and the research behind them are in the project repository: docs/architecture.md (why sails and pods), docs/future-tech.md (what hardware actually flies), docs/earth.md (what Earth allows), docs/data/ (every study's numbers and sources) and src/model/hybrid.js (the model, with tests). Photographs are path traced in Blender Cycles at the true solar irradiance for the orbit; thermal images map 8–14 µm band radiance to a fixed false-colour scale.
