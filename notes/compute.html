    Chips in the film

    The obvious way to build a solar-powered computer in space is a box of chips fed by solar wings and cooled by radiators. This swarm does something stranger: it spreads its chips through the solar film itself. Here is why, and what that computer can and cannot do.

    From docs/architecture.md, the workload and interconnect studies in docs/data/future/, and the page’s model · October 2026

    In short

    • Gathering chips into a box means carrying their heat away again. A box needs pumps, pipes and radiators, and these weigh more than the chips. Spread through the film, each chip’s heat is radiated within a few centimetres. Per kilogram, the film computes about 6.7 times as much as the best box.
    • The film is a capable computer. With light guides between its chips, one sail works as a single machine of about 15 MW, and it can train and run large AI models.
    • A few boxes, called pods, remain for work the film cannot host: memory that cannot survive unshielded radiation, chips too big to cool in the film, archives, and general-purpose computers for AI agents’ tools. They take about 6% of the power.
    • The swarm is a population, not one mind. A sail is the largest thing that computes as one. Beyond that, sails cooperate loosely, the way data centres do.

    Box or sheet?

    Computers like their parts close together. Short wires are fast and cheap, which is why an AI data centre packs tens of kilowatts into each rack. So the obvious design for a computer in space is the same: a box of chips at the centre, wings of solar cells feeding it, and radiators to get rid of its heat. Google’s Suncatcher study of 2025 is a design of this kind.

    The alternative sounds wasteful at first. Spread the chips through the solar film itself, one small chip for every few square centimetres of cell. Each chip runs on the power of the cell beneath it, and the film around it radiates its heat. Nothing is carried anywhere. The price is that the computer is now a sheet hundreds of metres across, with its parts far apart.

    The question was worked through in four studies, each with its own calculations: how data moves, what the work is, heat and mass and reliability, and finally an adversarial review that tried to break the first three. The review overturned several of their claims, and the numbers here are the corrected ones. They were then rechecked for the hardware the swarm will actually be built with, a century from now. The answer held both times: spread the chips out.

    Heat is the whole story

    A chip turns all its electricity into heat, and in space the only way to get rid of heat is to radiate it. Radiating takes area, roughly a square metre for every few hundred watts at the temperatures chips like. And sunlight arrives spread over just such an area. So the choice is between gathering the power into one place and then spreading the heat back out over radiators, or never gathering it at all.

    Gathering costs mass at every step. The coolant has to travel tens of metres, so a lot of it is needed. The pipes need armour, because interplanetary dust strikes at about 20 km/s, and a puncture in a main pipe shuts down half a megawatt of chips, where a puncture in the film loses one chip. And the radiators need heat pipes along their fins and must be held edge-on to the Sun. Altogether that is about 2 grams per watt in the best box anyone could design. The film needs none of it: its thin magnesium heat spreader costs a tenth of a gram per watt.

    With 2026 hardware the film computes 4.8 times as much per kilogram as the best pod. (An earlier pass had said ten times; the review found it had compared the film against a pod that was not the best one could build.) With the mature hardware in the model the gap widens to 6.7 times: the film gets thinner faster than the pod gets lighter, and the pod’s chips run hotter, at 105 °C, to keep its radiators small, which costs energy on every operation.

    A box gets nothing back for its trouble. One might hope it could place its solar wings where they work best, or run its radiators hot. At its own best design, though, a pod’s wings sit at the same distance from the Sun and the same temperature as the film. Running them closer in, where silicon makes the most power per square metre, adds 9% to the mass and wastes 40% of the light.

    Why the chips must be small

    If spreading the chips out is good, why not cluster them a little, a few big chips per square metre instead of hundreds of small ones? Because a thin sheet carries heat sideways badly. A bigger chip has to send its heat farther to reach enough film to radiate it, through thicker metal. The metal needed per watt grows in step with the chip’s power, so the total grows with its square.

    At 0.15 W a chip stays within 7–10 °C of the film around it. That number fixes everything else: one chip per 2.5 cm, about a hundred million per sail.

    Mercury’s elements nudge the design the same way. Spacecraft pumps today often use a sodium-potassium alloy as coolant, but Mercury has too little potassium to cool a swarm that way, and it has almost no hydrogen, so ammonia and water are out too. Pods use sulphur dioxide, which Mercury has in abundance. The film needs no coolant at all.

    What a sheet of chips can compute

    A sheet of chips is a grid. Each chip talks cheaply to its four neighbours, but a message to a chip on the far side of the sail has to hop from chip to chip, paying energy and time at each step. With only wires, a sail would be a loose patchwork of small computers rather than one big one.

    The mature design therefore lays light guides through the film: thin glass fibres or waveguides that run unbroken across the fold lines. With them, every chip can reach every other in a few microseconds, and the whole sail becomes one machine of about 15 MW and a hundred million chips, with one to two gigabytes of memory on each, an exabyte or more in all. That is a large data centre in one sheet.

    What can it run? Most of what AI data centres run today. Splitting each layer of a large model over a square patch of chips costs only a few per cent of the energy for running the model and a few to a dozen per cent for training it. Models built from many “experts”, where each word is routed to a few specialised parts of the model, cost more, because the routing sends data across the sail; with light guides that cost falls to 2–5%. The hardest part is keeping all the chips busy, since a part of the model held by one patch idles while the rest of the sail works on other things.

    How the film compares with a box of memory chips

    Against a pod built around today’s high-bandwidth memory, the film delivers 6–90 times as many words per second per kilogram when it serves a few users at a time, and 1.8–6 times at large batch sizes. Its advantage is greatest for producing one long stream of text quickly, which matters for AI agents working inside the swarm. Earth, up to 16 light-minutes away, gains little from it.

    With 2026 chips (16 MB of memory each), a model of half a trillion parameters needs a patch of film 3–10 m across, and the largest models of 4–10 trillion parameters need 17–100 m depending on how many users they serve. Mature chips hold about a hundred times more memory, so the same models fit in a patch a tenth the size.

    The sail is the unit

    Sails are separate spacecraft linked by lasers, and the lasers between two sails carry a small fraction of what the light guides inside one carry. Training one model across two sails in lockstep would need about 100 petabits a second of links per sail, against the 6 petabits its terminals provide. So the sail is the largest thing that computes as one machine.

    Beyond a single sail, cooperation is looser. Groups of a hundred to a hundred thousand sails could train one model together with methods that swap only averaged updates every few hundred steps, which needs a tiny fraction of the laser capacity. Whether such methods work at that scale is unproven: the largest public run of this kind, in 2026, used about 5×10²³ operations and up to 16 copies of the model. Across the whole swarm, messages relay through 60–70 hops and take 15–35 minutes, so the swarm as a whole syncs only on the scale of hours.

    What limits the size of a single model, in the end, is not hardware but whether anyone knows how to train one across thousands of loosely linked machines. A training run a hundred times today’s largest would take the finished swarm about five trillionths of a second.

    Why some boxes remain

    Some work does not fit a grid of small unshielded chips, and goes into pods:

    • Tools for AI agents. Agents write and run code, test it and browse, in walled-off computers that each need several gigabytes of ordinary memory and a general-purpose processor. Industry is buying more of those relative to AI chips every year.
    • Memory that cannot fly unshielded. Ordinary memory chips and flash storage, which hold datasets, checkpoints and archives, fail under the radiation an unshielded film collects. Pods carry 5 g/cm² of magnesium shielding around their memory.
    • Big chips. Anything above about 4 W cannot shed its heat into the film.

    How much of the power goes to pods depends almost entirely on one choice. If every chip in the film is given a processor able to run agents safely walled off from each other, pods fall to about 1%; that capability costs 10–30% of the swarm’s mass. If no chip can, pods stay near 15% for good. The model takes 6%.

    A pod is a 1 MW block of memory stacked on logic, about 1.8 m across, inside its shield. Two solar wings of 68 m feed it, and four radiator blades, 40 m long and only 25 mm thick, shed its heat. The blades are held exactly edge-on to the Sun, so sunlight never falls on their faces.

    Pods cannot simply ride along with the sails they serve. Sunlight pushes the light film about ten times harder, for its weight, than a heavy pod, so the two drift apart by about a kilometre a second each year. Pods fly in their own formations within the layer of sails, close enough to reach them by laser, and kept off the sails’ lines to the Sun so that their shadows fall on nothing.

    What the swarm computes

    Finished, the swarm runs about 1.9×10⁴⁰ operations a second. That is about 6×10¹⁷ times all the AI chips in the world in 2025. At a common estimate of 10¹⁵ operations a second for a human brain (Carlsmith 2020, uncertain a hundredfold either way), it is the computing of about 2×10²⁵ brains.

    A single sail with hardware of around 2070 could serve about 13 million AI agents at once, or train in a year a model with about ten trillion active parameters. Such a run would need between 1.6 and 7 times all the text people have ever written, so most of what the swarm learns from would have to be generated: simulations, self-play, experiments.

    So the swarm is best thought of as a population: 10²³–10²⁵ agents working at once, with searches, simulations and experiments running in parallel. Whether that population can be organised into something more coherent is a question about algorithms, not about the hardware described here.

    What remains open

    • Training across many loosely linked machines. Proven so far only to about 5×10²³ operations and 16 copies.
    • Chips that can run agents safely. Whether every chip in the film can, decides whether pods are 1% or 15% of the swarm.
    • What the demand will be. Running models has overtaken training them, from about a quarter of AI computing in 2024 to about two thirds in 2026. The design does not depend on it, because the film can also train.
    • Energy per operation. The research files disagree by up to 3.5 times. The model uses one consistent basis for each era (Hardware after 2095).
