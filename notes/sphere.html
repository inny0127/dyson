    Closing the sphere

    A swarm that catches part of the Sun is easy to picture. One that catches all of it has to solve three problems: its sails fly on orbits that must cross, a closed shell can only lose heat outward, and Earth still needs its sunlight.

    From docs/closed-swarm.md and the page’s model · October 2026

    In short

    • Two layers. Computing sails cover the sphere at 0.95 AU from the Sun, except a patch round the line to Earth. There, mirrors at 0.98 AU keep pace with Earth, pass their sunlight back to the sails, and show Earth only their cold backs.
    • Orbits must cross, and that costs π/2. Every orbit circles the Sun, and any two such circles cross. A theorem proved in 2017 says the cheapest way to cover a sphere with orbiting bands still uses π/2, about 1.57, times the sphere’s area. Sails that pass behind others turn edge-on and wait.
    • A closed sphere runs hot. All of the Sun’s power has to leave through the outside, so the sails reach 419 K and their chips about 155 °C. They compute about half as much per watt as cool ones.
    • Closing it is about control, not computing. It computes slightly less than an open swarm would. What it buys is that every bit of light leaving the inner solar system goes out through a window the swarm chooses to open.

    Two layers

    The computing sails described in the sail note cover the whole sphere at 0.95 AU, just inside Earth’s orbit, all facing the Sun. They leave out one patch: the part of the sky round the line from the Sun to Earth, 23° in every direction, 4% of the sphere.

    That patch is filled by a second layer, slightly farther out at 0.98 AU: mirrors that travel round the Sun with Earth. Their inner faces reflect sunlight back into the sphere, where the computing sails catch it. Their outer faces, which are all of the swarm that Earth can see once it is closed, are cold. In the middle of the mirrors is Earth’s window, a hole where the mirrors in Earth’s line of sight have turned edge-on.

    Why mirrors there, rather than computing sails with something to hide their backs? A computing sail’s back is at 419 K, and Earth would receive about 540 W per square metre from a patch of them, more than twice the sunlight it absorbs. Insulating their backs would push them to 489 K, too hot for chips. Mirrors lose nothing: they hand their light on to the sails behind them, which catch it one bounce later.

    Why close it at all?

    An open swarm can be large without enclosing anything, and the page’s earlier design did just that. It left the sky round Earth’s line of sight empty, filled the rest, and caught 83% of the Sun. It worked, at a cost to Earth: the swarm’s heat on Earth added up to twelve times what doubling the air’s CO₂ would, and cancelling it took a sunshade blocking a fifth of Earth’s sunlight and costing about 30% of the world’s rain (Earth inside the swarm).

    Closing the sphere removes both problems. Earth sees only the mirrors’ cold backs, 0.56 W per square metre, which is cancelled by narrowing its window by a quarter of a per cent. And it keeps its full sunlight. The computing hardly changes: the closed sphere catches 18% more light, but its hotter chips compute 19% less per watt, so it ends 5% below the open swarm.

    So the reason to close it is control. Once closed, every bit of light leaving the inner solar system leaves through a window the swarm chooses to open, and Earth’s sunlight becomes a dial.

    Orbits must cross

    Here is a problem that sounds like a technicality and turns out to cost a third of the swarm. Every orbit round the Sun lies in a plane through the Sun, so seen from the Sun it traces a great circle on the sky, like the equator. Any two great circles cross, at two opposite points. So sails covering every direction must pass one another somewhere, and where they do, one is behind the other, in its shadow.

    A sail in another’s shadow cannot just stay where it is, face-on. It would get no light, and it would sit right behind the lit sail, trapping that sail’s heat. So it turns edge-on and waits until it is clear, the same move that opens Earth’s window. The real cost is the sails themselves: more must be flown than are lit at any moment.

    How many more? Fly the sails in bands, each band following one great circle round the Sun. In 1973 the mathematician László Fejes Tóth conjectured that bands covering a sphere must have widths adding up to at least half the sphere’s circumference. Zilin Jiang and Alexandr Polyanskii proved it in 2017. That puts the total area of the bands at no less than π/2, about 1.57, times the sphere’s area. And one arrangement reaches that bound exactly: bands that all cross at the same two points, like the lines of longitude on a globe all meeting at the poles.

    While the sphere is still filling, the overlap is small. Bands crossing at the same points only begin to overlap near those points, so at 10% of the Sun they fly 1.02 sails per sail lit, at half 1.13, at 99% 1.51, and closed π/2. Orbits left to chance would do far worse, needing several times as many sails to close the last gaps.

    The formula

    Bands crossing at the same two points that cover a share f₀ of the circle halfway between those points cover a share

    F = f₀ · arccos f₀ + 1 − √(1 − f₀²)

    of the sphere, using πf₀/2 of the sphere’s area in bands. The model solves this for f₀ at each coverage (bandArea). Orbits at random, by contrast, cover 1 − e−A of the sphere with area A, so covering a share F takes −ln(1 − F) times the sphere: 4.6 times at 99%.

    The cost at closure: 5.2×10¹⁸ sails flown, of which 3.3×10¹⁸ are lit at any moment, and 5.67×10²² kg in all, 57% more than if sails could sit still. The computing is unchanged, since the same light falls on the same number of working chips. The extra mass is why Mercury’s iron core has to be mined (Taking Mercury apart).

    Flying in bands

    Each band is a formation of sails on side-by-side orbits. A sail on its own would follow its own great circle; to hold a band tens of thousands of kilometres wide, the sails away from the band’s centre line lean 3–4°, so that sunlight pushes them slightly off their natural path. That costs 0.2% of their light. With bands about 70,000 km wide, about 6,400 of them close the sphere.

    Where bands cross they pass about a kilometre apart, so the whole computing shell is about 7,000 km thick, a twenty-thousandth of its radius. That thinness matters. While the sphere is filling, a gap in one band lines up with the gaps behind it even for slanting lines of sight, so the sphere lets through exactly what it should. And because the Sun is half a degree wide, its light arrives at a slight slant; across a layer 7,000 km thick that shifts a shadow by only about 30 km, small beside bands 70,000 km wide.

    The model does not simulate the flying itself: thousands of bands passing a kilometre apart, each sail turning edge-on at the right moment, for centuries. Those are engineering problems of a familiar kind on an unfamiliar scale.

    Why it runs hot

    A lone sail sheds heat from both faces. A closed sphere cannot: every front faces another front across the hollow middle, and whatever one gives off, another absorbs. So in balance all of the Sun’s power has to leave through the outside, through the sails’ backs.

    That sets the temperature. The Sun’s 3.8×10²⁶ W leaving through a sphere 0.95 AU in radius, from backs that radiate 90% as well as a perfect black body, gives 419 K. A sail alone at the same distance would run at 332 K. Between sparse and closed, the sails warm steadily as the gaps close.

    Farther from the Sun the shell would be cooler, but it cannot go farther. It must stay inside Earth’s orbit, which comes within 0.983 AU of the Sun, and leave room outside it for the mirrors; and enclosing Earth would trap it inside a shell at about 330 K. So 0.95 AU is as far out as the shell can be, and it pays for that: chips at about 155 °C, solar cells converting over a quarter less than at 85 °C, and logic spending half as much again on every operation. Each watt of sunlight computes 48% of what it would on a cool sail.

    The arithmetic

    All of the Sun’s output leaves through the backs:

    L☉ = 4πr² (1 − 0.04) · εback σ T⁴

    With L☉ = 3.828×10²⁶ W, r = 0.95 AU, 4% of the sphere left to the mirrors and εback = 0.9, T = 419 K. A perfectly black back would still run at 408 K. A second, colder shell catching this one’s heat (a so-called Matrioshka brain) could run at 100 K from 15.6 AU, but it is a different machine with far more mass, and not in the model.

    How it fills

    The page shows the sphere at seven stages. At every one it is the same sphere, equally full in every direction, with a share f of the Sun’s light falling on sails. That keeps Earth’s view simple, and it is how a swarm limited by its rate of production would naturally grow. Three rules hold throughout: the patch in front of Earth is always mirrors; any sail crossing the line from the Sun to Earth turns edge-on as it passes; and while the sphere is open, the sails’ fronts are switched to radiate poorly in the infrared, because Earth sees them through the gaps.

    The mirrors in front of Earth

    The mirrors have to travel round the Sun once a year, keeping pace with Earth. Anything orbiting freely at 0.98 AU would go round faster, so they need help: sunlight pushing outward on them must carry part of their weight, exactly enough to slow their orbit to Earth’s pace. The share works out to 1 − 0.98³, about 6%. For a mirror that fixes how heavy it can be: 26 grams per square metre.

    Each mirror flies its own slightly tilted orbit, so that it drifts north and south across the patch once a year, and the mirrors are spaced so that the patch stays full. Its edge is soft over about a degree. Because they share Earth’s slightly oval orbit, the mirrors stay about 3 million km sunward of Earth all year.

    The mirrors are aluminium. Silver reflects better, but coating 10²² square metres would take far more silver than Mercury has, while aluminium is plentiful. Each mirror is 40 layers of aluminium foil a quarter of a micrometre thick: the innermost, under a thin coating, reflects 96% of sunlight, and the rest insulate. The inner face runs at about 500 K, but so little heat gets through the stack that the outer face stays at about 170 K. Earth receives 0.56 W per square metre from it, averaged over the globe. In all, the mirrors weigh 4.4×10²⁰ kg, under 1% of the swarm.

    Windows

    A window is a group of mirrors turned edge-on while they cross a line of sight. Earth’s has to be wide enough that every point on Earth sees the whole Sun through it, and the Sun is not a point.

    The mirrors drift across the Sun–Earth line at up to 12 km/s, so each spends about an hour in Earth’s window, 40,600 km across, before it turns back. Each turns edge-on in about ten minutes, using a flywheel of about 50 kg, and starts turning before it reaches the window’s edge. Sunlight alone could turn a mirror, through strips on its surface that change how much they reflect, but only over a couple of hours, and it stops pushing once the mirror is edge-on. No mirror uses any propellant.

    The Moon gets its own window, 15,700 km in radius, which crosses the mirrors once a month. Mars, much farther beyond the shell, needs one 260,000 km in radius, still under a millionth of the swarm’s light. A spacecraft or telescope needs a pinhole. Altogether, Earth’s and the Moon’s windows let out less than a hundred-millionth of the Sun’s light, and the mirrors’ cold backs about four hundred-thousandths. Everything else is caught.

    The alternative: a shade near Earth

    All Earth needs is that every line of sight from it toward the swarm ends on something cold. The mirrors do that from inside the swarm. A shade much closer to Earth would do it too, and could be far smaller, since its size shrinks with its distance from Earth.

    Such a shade could hover about a million kilometres sunward of Earth, held up by the push of the swarm’s own heat, and would need to be about 6 million km across to hide the sphere: 500 times lighter than the mirrors. It would cast sharper shadows, too. But it is opaque, and it would have to go up early in the build, before the sails’ hot backs facing Earth added several watts per square metre. From then on Earth’s sky within 72° of the Sun would be black for most of the build, where with the mirrors it glows (Earth inside the swarm). That is why the page keeps the mirrors.

    Why not a filter that passes sunlight and reflects heat?

    A shade that let visible light through and reflected the swarm’s infrared would let Earth keep its sunlight without a window. The best heat-reflecting coatings reflect about 97% of it. The 3% they absorb, about 43 W per square metre, warms the filter, which radiates half of it toward Earth: about 7 W per square metre averaged over the globe, more than doubling the air’s CO₂ would add. More layers would block more heat but also the sunlight. And a filter that turns infrared away would also turn back Earth’s own heat along the same paths.

    The rest of the solar system

    Venus is inside the sphere. It can no longer cool to the cold of space, only to a sky at 419 K, and its temperature climbs; it needs a window or a shade of its own to stay as it is. Mars receives the shell’s heat instead of sunlight, about the same energy as today, and warms by about 12 °C; a window toward it would add sunlight on top. Icy moons absorb infrared far better than sunlight, so Europa warms by about 30 °C. And the solar wind stops, absorbed by the shell everywhere but the windows (Earth inside the swarm).

    Open questions

    • Formation flying at this scale. Thousands of bands a kilometre apart, each sail turning at the right moment, for centuries, and the capture of any sail that fails.
    • Keeping the mirrors in step. Billions of tilted orbits phased so the patch stays full, under the pull of Earth and the Moon.
    • Switchable infrared fronts over 10²³ square metres of film while the sphere is open.
    • The temperature in between. The model eases the temperature from sparse to closed; a full calculation of radiation inside a partly built sphere would sharpen the middle stages.
